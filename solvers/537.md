# Project Euler 537 Solution - Counting Tuples

<https://projecteuler.net/problem=537>:

* [537.py](537.py)

## Problem Summary

Let $\pi(x)$ denote the number of [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) not exceeding $x$, and let $p_r$ be the $r$-th [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime). The problem asks for the number $T(n,k)$ of ordered $k$-tuples of [positive integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/positive-integer) $(x_1,\dots,x_k)$ such that

$$\pi(x_1)+\pi(x_2)+\cdots+\pi(x_k)=n,$$

with the final result taken [modulo](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modulo-operation)

$$M=1004535809.$$

The decisive observation is that many different [integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) share the same [prime-counting function](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-counting-function) value, and those multiplicities are controlled exactly by consecutive [prime gaps](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-gap).

## Mathematical Approach

The implementations turn the tuple-counting problem into [coefficient extraction](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/coefficient-extraction) from a [generating function](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/generating-function), and then accelerate the [polynomial](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/polynomial) arithmetic with an [NTT](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-transform).

### Step 1: Group Integers by Their Prime-Count Value

For $r=0$, there is only one [positive integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/positive-integer) with $\pi(x)=0$, namely $x=1$. Therefore

$$a_0=1.$$

For every $r\ge 1$, the condition $\pi(x)=r$ means that $x$ lies between the $r$-th and $(r+1)$-st [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime):

$$\pi(x)=r \iff p_r \le x \le p_{r+1}-1.$$

So the number of such [integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) is exactly the [prime gap](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-gap)

$$a_r=p_{r+1}-p_r \qquad (r\ge 1).$$

These coefficients are the basic weights of the whole problem: $a_r$ counts how many choices one coordinate has if it contributes exactly $r$ to the total sum of [prime-counting function](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-counting-function) values.

### Step 2: Write the Tuple Recurrence

If the first coordinate contributes $i$, there are $a_i$ possible values for it, and the remaining $k-1$ coordinates must contribute $n-i$. This gives the [convolution](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/convolution) [recurrence relation](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/recurrence-relation)

$$T(n,k)=\sum_{i=0}^{n} a_i\,T(n-i,k-1).$$

The natural [initial conditions](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/initial-condition) are

$$T(0,0)=1,\qquad T(n,0)=0 \text{ for } n>0,$$

and for one coordinate we simply have

$$T(n,1)=a_n.$$

So the [combinatorics](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/combinatorics) is already telling us that each extra coordinate adds one more [discrete convolution](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/discrete-convolution) with the [sequence](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/sequence) $(a_0,a_1,a_2,\dots)$.

### Step 3: Convert the Recurrence into a Generating Function

Introduce the [ordinary generating function](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/ordinary-generating-function)

$$A(x)=\sum_{r\ge 0} a_r x^r$$

and, for fixed $k$,

$$F_k(x)=\sum_{n\ge 0} T(n,k)x^n.$$

The [recurrence relation](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/recurrence-relation) from the previous step becomes

$$F_k(x)=A(x)\,F_{k-1}(x).$$

Iterating this identity and using $F_0(x)=1$, we get

$$F_k(x)=A(x)^k.$$

Therefore the answer is the coefficient of $x^n$:

$$\boxed{T(n,k)=[x^n]A(x)^k.}$$

This is the exact mathematical statement implemented by the C++, Python, and Java versions.

### Step 4: Truncation Is Safe

When we only need $[x^n]A(x)^k$, no coefficient of degree greater than $n$ can ever become relevant later. Every multiplication adds nonnegative exponents, so once a term has degree $>n$, future multiplications can only push it to even higher degree.

Hence after every [polynomial](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/polynomial) product we may safely discard all terms above degree $n$ and keep only

$$c_0+c_1x+\cdots+c_nx^n.$$

This is why the implementations always work with truncated [polynomials](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/polynomial) of length $n+1$, even while repeatedly squaring during [exponentiation](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/exponentiation).

### Step 5: Use an NTT-Friendly Modulus

A naive [convolution](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/convolution) of two degree-$n$ [polynomials](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/polynomial) costs $O(n^2)$, which is too slow for the target size. The chosen [integer modulus](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer-modulus) is

$$M=1004535809=479\cdot 2^{21}+1.$$

Because $M-1$ contains a large power of $2$, every transform length

$$L=2^m \le 2^{21}$$

has the necessary primitive $L$-th [roots of unity](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/roots-of-unity) [modulo](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modulo-operation) $M$. In particular, the [primitive root](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/primitive-root) $3$ generates all the roots needed by the transform. So [polynomial](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/polynomial) multiplication can be done exactly [modulo](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modulo-operation) $M$ with a [Number Theoretic Transform](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-transform) in

$$O(L\log L)$$

time instead of quadratic time.

### Worked Example: $T(3,2)$

The first few coefficient buckets are easy to read off from the [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime):

$$a_0=1,\qquad a_1=3-2=1,\qquad a_2=5-3=2,\qquad a_3=7-5=2.$$

Indeed, the corresponding sets are

$$\pi(x)=0 \Rightarrow \{1\},\qquad \pi(x)=1 \Rightarrow \{2\},\qquad \pi(x)=2 \Rightarrow \{3,4\},\qquad \pi(x)=3 \Rightarrow \{5,6\}.$$

Now

$$T(3,2)=[x^3]A(x)^2=a_0a_3+a_1a_2+a_2a_1+a_3a_0=2+2+2+2=8.$$

The eight [ordered pairs](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/ordered-pair) are

$$(1,5),(1,6),(2,3),(2,4),(3,2),(4,2),(5,1),(6,1).$$

This small example shows exactly why [coefficient extraction](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/coefficient-extraction) matches the tuple count.

## How the Code Works

The implementations first generate enough consecutive [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) to know the gaps $p_{r+1}-p_r$ for all $0\le r\le n$. From those gaps they build the truncated coefficient list of $A(x)$ up to degree $n$.

[Polynomial](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/polynomial) multiplication is then performed by padding both coefficient arrays to the next [power of two](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/power-of-two), applying the forward [NTT](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-transform), multiplying pointwise, and applying the inverse [NTT](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-transform). After every product, the result is truncated back to degree $n$.

The Python implementation keeps the [NTT](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-transform) buffers large enough for the full product before truncating, so higher coefficients cannot wrap around into the low degrees during the transform.

To obtain $A(x)^k$, the implementations use [binary exponentiation](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/exponentiation-by-squaring) in the [polynomial ring](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/polynomial-ring): when the current bit of $k$ is set, the running result is multiplied by the current base; after that, the base is squared. Because every intermediate product is truncated, the degree never grows beyond what is needed.

Once the [exponentiation](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/exponentiation) finishes, the coefficient of $x^n$ is returned [modulo](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modulo-operation) $M$.

## Complexity Analysis

Let $B$ be a [number-theoretic sieve](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-sieve) bound large enough to contain $p_{n+1}$, and let $L$ be the smallest [power of two](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/power-of-two) satisfying $L\ge 2n+1$. Generating the needed [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) by a [number-theoretic sieve](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-sieve) costs $O(B\log\log B)$ time and $O(B)$ memory.

Each truncated [polynomial](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/polynomial) multiplication costs $O(L\log L)$, and [binary exponentiation](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/exponentiation-by-squaring) performs $O(\log k)$ such multiplications. Therefore the full running time is

$$O(B\log\log B + L\log L\log k).$$

Since $L=O(n)$, the [polynomial](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/polynomial) part is usually summarized as

$$O(n\log n\log k).$$

The memory usage is $O(B+L)$ while the [number-theoretic sieve](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-sieve) and [polynomial](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/polynomial) buffers coexist.
