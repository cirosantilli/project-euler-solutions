# Project Euler 358 Solution - Cyclic Numbers

<https://projecteuler.net/problem=358>:

* [358.py](358.py)

## Problem Summary

We seek the unique [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) $p$ such that the [decimal expansion](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/decimal-expansion) of $1/p$ begins with the [digits](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) $00000000137$ and the full repetend defines a cyclic number whose last five [digits](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) are $56789$. Once that [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) is identified, the required Project Euler quantity is the sum of the [digits](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) of the cyclic number.

## Mathematical Approach

### Step 1: Write the repetend as an integer

For a [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) $p \neq 2,5$, the [decimal expansion](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/decimal-expansion) of $1/p$ is purely repeating. If the period is maximal, then its length is $p-1$, and the repeating block can be written as

$$N=\frac{10^{p-1}-1}{p}.$$

The number $N$ is the [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) formed by the repeating [digits](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) of $1/p$. When $p$ is a full reptend [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) in base $10$, the multiples $N,2N,\dots,(p-1)N$ are cyclic rotations of the same [digit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) string, which is exactly why the problem is about cyclic numbers.

### Step 2: Convert the decimal prefix into an interval for $p$

The first eleven [digits](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) after the decimal point are $00000000137$. Multiplying $1/p$ by $10^{11}$ therefore gives a number whose [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) part is $137$:

$$137 \le \frac{10^{11}}{p} \lt 138.$$

Rearranging yields the narrow [interval](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/interval)

$$\frac{10^{11}}{138} \lt p \le \frac{10^{11}}{137}.$$

This is implemented directly as

$$\texttt{low}=\left\lfloor\frac{10^{11}}{138}\right\rfloor+1,\qquad \texttt{high}=\left\lfloor\frac{10^{11}}{137}\right\rfloor.$$

Numerically, this is $724637682 \lt p \le 729927007$, so the prefix already limits the search to a window of about $5.29\times 10^6$.

### Step 3: Convert the suffix into a congruence

The problem also tells us that the cyclic number ends in $56789$. Therefore

$$N \equiv 56789 \pmod{10^5}.$$

Since $pN=10^{p-1}-1$, reducing [modulo](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modulo) $10^5$ gives

$$pN \equiv -1 \pmod{10^5}.$$

Substituting the known suffix of $N$,

$$p \cdot 56789 \equiv -1 \pmod{10^5}.$$

Solving this [integer congruence](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer-congruence) yields

$$p \equiv 9891 \pmod{10^5}.$$

So every valid [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) must lie in one [arithmetic progression](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/arithmetic-progression) inside the [interval](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/interval). In the actual search window, that leaves exactly $53$ candidates.

### Step 4: Enforce the full-reptend condition

Not every [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) in that progression has a repetend of length $p-1$. For [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) $p \neq 2,5$, the period of $1/p$ in base $10$ is the [multiplicative order](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/multiplicative-order)

$$\operatorname{ord}_p(10).$$

By [Fermat's little theorem](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/fermat-s-little-theorem), this order divides $p-1$. Therefore the period is maximal exactly when

$$\operatorname{ord}_p(10)=p-1.$$

The standard test used by all three local solutions is: factor $p-1$ into distinct [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) [divisors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) $q$, and verify that

$$10^{(p-1)/q} \not\equiv 1 \pmod p$$

for every such $q$. If any one of these [integer congruences](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer-congruence) equals $1$, then the order is a [proper divisor](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/proper-divisor) of $p-1$, so the candidate is rejected. In this repository's search, the [arithmetic progression](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/arithmetic-progression) gives $53$ values, only $3$ of them are [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime), and only one survives the order test:

$$p=729809891.$$

### Step 5: Derive the digit-sum formula

Let $s$ be the sum of the [digits](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) of the cyclic number $N$. Because $N,2N,\dots,(p-1)N$ are cyclic rotations, each of these numbers has the same [digit sum](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/digit-sum) $s$. Summing them as multiples gives

$$\sum_{k=1}^{p-1} kN = N \cdot \frac{p(p-1)}{2}.$$

Summing the same [set](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/set) column by column gives a [repunit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/repunit) times the [digit sum](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/digit-sum):

$$\sum_{k=1}^{p-1} kN = s \cdot R_{p-1},\qquad R_{p-1}=\frac{10^{p-1}-1}{9}.$$

Since $pN=10^{p-1}-1$, we also have $R_{p-1}=pN/9$. Cancelling $N$ yields

$$s=\frac{9(p-1)}{2}.$$

Substituting the unique [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) found above gives

$$s=\frac{9(729809891-1)}{2}=3284144505.$$

This is the value returned by the local implementations.

## How the Code Works

The solver first computes the exact [interval](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/interval) forced by the leading [digits](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit). It then solves the suffix [integer congruence](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer-congruence) with a [modular inverse](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modular-inverse), aligns the first candidate [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) to that [residue class](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/residue-class) [modulo](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modulo) $10^5$, and advances through the resulting [arithmetic progression](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/arithmetic-progression). This replaces the old broad [number-theoretic sieve](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-sieve) with a scan of only a few dozen [integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer).

For each candidate, `is_prime` uses [trial division](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/trial-division) by numbers of the form $6k\pm 1$. That simple test is enough after the [interval](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/interval) and [integer congruence](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer-congruence) filters. If the candidate is [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime), `distinct_prime_factors` factors $p-1$, and `is_full_reptend_prime` checks that no [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) [divisor](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) of $p-1$ gives a smaller [multiplicative order](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/multiplicative-order) for $10$ [modulo](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modulo) $p$.

The prefix condition is also kept as an [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) guard,

$$\left\lfloor\frac{10^{11}}{p}\right\rfloor=137$$

so no [floating-point](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/floating-point-representation) comparison is involved. Once the unique full-reptend [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) has been found, the code returns the [digit sum](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/digit-sum) from the closed formula $9(p-1)/2$ rather than constructing the enormous repetend.

## Complexity Analysis

Let $C$ be the number of candidates after the [interval](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/interval) and [integer congruence](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer-congruence) filters. Here $C=53$. Each [primality](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/primality) or factorization pass costs $O(\sqrt{p})$ in the worst case, and each order check adds one [modular exponentiation](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modular-exponentiation) per distinct [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) [divisor](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) of $p-1$. In practice the filtered search is tiny: only three candidates are [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime), and only one has maximal [decimal period](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/decimal-period).
