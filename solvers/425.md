# Project Euler 425 Solution - Prime Connection

<https://projecteuler.net/problem=425>:

* [425.py](425.py)

## Problem Summary

Fix a limit $L$. We look at the [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) up to $L$, and we connect two [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) when one legal decimal edit turns one into the other while staying [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime). The allowed edits are: replace one [digit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) without creating a leading zero, delete the most significant [digit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit), or prepend one new nonzero [digit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit).

The required sum is not over all unreachable [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) in the full [graph](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/graph). For each [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) $p$, we only allow [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) $\le p$ as intermediate [vertices](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/vertex-of-a-graph). A [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) contributes exactly when 2 is still disconnected from $p$ inside that restricted [graph](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/graph).

## Mathematical Approach

### Step 1: The Prime Edit Graph

Let $\mathbb{P}$ denote the set of [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) and define

$$V_L=\mathbb{P}\cap [2,L].$$

We build an [undirected graph](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/undirected-graph) $G_L=(V_L,E_L)$. An [edge](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/edge-of-a-graph) joins two [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) if one can be obtained from the other by exactly one allowed decimal edit. Replacing a [digit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) is symmetric, and deleting the leading [digit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) is the inverse of prepending a leading [digit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit), so the [graph](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/graph) may be treated as undirected.

If a [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) has $d$ [digits](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit), then its neighbors are generated by three families of moves:

1\. change one of the $d$ [digits](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) to another [digit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit), with the restriction that the first [digit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) cannot become 0;

2\. if $d \ge 2$, remove the first [digit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit);

3\. prepend one [digit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) from 1 through 9.

Only [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) results remain in the [graph](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/graph).

### Step 2: Reformulate the Condition with Threshold Graphs

For every threshold $t \le L$, let $G_{\le t}$ be the [induced subgraph](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/induced-subgraph) on

$$V_t=\mathbb{P}\cap [2,t].$$

Write $2 \sim_t p$ when 2 and $p$ are connected in $G_{\le t}$. Then the problem asks for

$$S(L)=\sum_{\substack{p\in\mathbb{P}\\ p\le L\\ 2 \not\sim_p p}} p.$$

So each [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) has its own threshold: we do not ask whether $p$ is connected to 2 somewhere in the full [graph](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/graph) up to $L$, but whether it is already connected at the moment the [graph](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/graph) is truncated at $p$.

### Step 3: Convert the Problem to a Minimax Path Value

Define $\tau(p)$ as the smallest threshold $t$ such that $2 \sim_t p$. Equivalently, for any [graph path](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/graph-path)

$$P=(q_0=2,q_1,\dots,q_k=p),$$

define its bottleneck value by

$$B(P)=\max_{0\le i\le k} q_i.$$

Then

$$\tau(p)=\min_{P:2\leadsto p} B(P).$$

This identity is the key reduction. If a [graph path](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/graph-path) has bottleneck $M$, then every [vertex](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/vertex-of-a-graph) of that [graph path](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/graph-path) lies in $G_{\le M}$, so $2 \sim_M p$. Conversely, if $2 \sim_t p$, some [graph path](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/graph-path) in $G_{\le t}$ connects them, and that [graph path](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/graph-path) has maximum [vertex](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/vertex-of-a-graph) at most $t$. Therefore the minimum threshold and the minimum bottleneck are the same quantity.

The contribution test now becomes simply

$$\tau(p)>p.$$

In words: every [graph path](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/graph-path) from 2 to $p$ is forced to pass through some [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) strictly larger than $p$.

### Step 4: Dijkstra with a Bottleneck Relaxation

The [minimax](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/minimax) value $\tau(p)$ can be computed by the same greedy structure as [Dijkstra's algorithm](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/dijkstra-s-algorithm). Instead of ordinary [path length](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/path-length), each [vertex](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/vertex-of-a-graph) stores its best known bottleneck value. Start with label 2 at the [vertex](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/vertex-of-a-graph) 2 and $+\infty$ elsewhere.

Suppose a [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) $u$ has already been reached with current label $d(u)$, and $v$ is a [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) neighbor of $u$. Any [graph path](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/graph-path) to $u$ with bottleneck $d(u)$ extends to a [graph path](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/graph-path) to $v$ with bottleneck

$$\max(d(u),v).$$

Hence the relaxation rule is

$$d(v)\leftarrow \min\bigl(d(v),\max(d(u),v)\bigr).$$

This works because extending a [graph path](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/graph-path) cannot decrease its bottleneck. The labels are monotone, so once the smallest tentative label is extracted from the [priority queue](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/priority-queue), no later [graph path](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/graph-path) can improve it. That is exactly the usual correctness argument for Dijkstra, with addition replaced by $\max$.

### Worked Example

The [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) 11 is the first nontrivial example. There is a [graph path](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/graph-path)

$$2 \to 3 \to 13 \to 11,$$

so $\tau(11)\le 13$. On the other hand, in the threshold [graph](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/graph) $G_{\le 11}$, the [vertex](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/vertex-of-a-graph) 11 has no valid incident [edge](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/edge-of-a-graph): every legal one-step edit gives either a non-prime, a number with a leading zero, or a [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) greater than 11. Therefore $2 \not\sim_{11} 11$, and

$$\tau(11)=13>11.$$

So 11 contributes to the sum.

The same [minimax](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/minimax) search shows

$$\tau(101)=\tau(103)=\tau(107)=\tau(109)=113.$$

Hence the contributing [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) below $10^3$ are

$$11,\ 101,\ 103,\ 107,\ 109,$$

and therefore

$$S(10^3)=11+101+103+107+109=431.$$

This matches the checkpoint used by the implementation. A second checkpoint is

$$S(10^4)=78728.$$

## How the Code Works

The C++, Python, and Java implementations first build a [sieve of Eratosthenes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/sieve-of-eratosthenes) up to $L$, so [primality tests](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/primality-test) become constant-time table lookups. They also precompute powers of 10 in order to address decimal positions directly.

The [graph](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/graph) is never materialized in full. Instead, whenever the [priority queue](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/priority-queue) extracts a [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime), the implementation generates all valid one-step edits of its [decimal representation](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/decimal-representation): same-length [digit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) replacements, deletion of the most significant [digit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit), and prepending of one nonzero [digit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit). Every candidate is tested against the [number-theoretic sieve](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-sieve), and [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) candidates are relaxed with the bottleneck update above.

After the queue is exhausted, each [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) $p$ has its final threshold value $\tau(p)$. The program then scans the [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) list once and accumulates exactly those [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) with $\tau(p)>p$.

## Complexity Analysis

Let

$$V=\pi(L),\qquad D=\lfloor \log_{10} L \rfloor + 1.$$

The [number-theoretic sieve](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-sieve) costs $O(L\log\log L)$ time and $O(L)$ memory. For each extracted [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime), neighbor generation inspects at most $9D$ replacements, one deletion, and up to nine prepends, so the implicit [graph](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/graph) contributes $E=O(VD)$ candidate relaxations. The [priority queue](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/priority-queue) phase therefore costs

$$O(E\log V)=O(VD\log V).$$

The total complexity is

$$O(L\log\log L + VD\log V)$$

time and $O(L)$ memory, which is efficient enough for the required limit.
