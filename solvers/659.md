# Project Euler 659 Solution - Largest Prime

<https://projecteuler.net/problem=659>:

* [659.py](659.py)

## Problem Summary

For each [positive integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/positive-integer) $k$, define

$$N_k=4k^2+1,$$

and let $P(k)$ denote the [largest prime factor](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/largest-prime-factor) of $N_k$. The goal is to compute

$$S(K)=\sum_{k=1}^{K} P(k)\pmod{10^{18}},\qquad K=10^7.$$

Factoring each $N_k$ independently would be far too slow, so the efficient approach is to [number-theoretic sieve](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-sieve) [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) [divisors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) across all $k$ at once.

## Mathematical Approach

The key observation is that a [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) [divisor](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) of $4k^2+1$ forces $k$ into very specific [residue classes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/residue-class). That turns the problem into a structured [number-theoretic sieve](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-sieve) on [arithmetic progressions](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/arithmetic-progression) rather than $10^7$ separate [integer factorizations](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer-factorization).

### Step 1: Restrict the Shape of the Prime Divisors

If a [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) $p$ divides $4k^2+1$, then

$$4k^2\equiv -1\pmod p,$$

so $-1$ must be a [quadratic residue](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/quadratic-residue) [modulo](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modulo) $p$. Since $4k^2+1$ is always odd, $p=2$ never occurs. For an [odd prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/odd-prime), the existence of a solution to

$$x^2\equiv -1\pmod p$$

implies

$$p\equiv 1\pmod 4.$$

Therefore only [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) congruent to $1 \bmod 4$ can appear as [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) [divisors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) of the numbers in this family.

### Step 2: Convert a Prime Divisor into Residue Classes of $k$

Fix an [odd prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/odd-prime) $p\equiv 1\pmod 4$. Once we find a [square root](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/square-root) $x$ of $-1$ [modulo](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modulo) $p$, we have

$$x^2\equiv -1\pmod p.$$

Because $4k^2=(2k)^2$, the [divisibility](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisibility) condition $p\mid 4k^2+1$ is equivalent to

$$2k\equiv \pm x \pmod p.$$

Since $2$ is invertible [modulo](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modulo) every [odd prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/odd-prime), this becomes

$$k\equiv \pm x\cdot 2^{-1}\pmod p.$$

So each eligible [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) contributes at most two [arithmetic progressions](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/arithmetic-progression) in $k$. The implementations use [Tonelli-Shanks](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/tonelli-shanks-algorithm) to obtain the modular [square root](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/square-root) and then sweep exactly those two progressions.

### Step 3: Remove the Full Prime Power

When a progression reaches an index $k$, the current value attached to that index is checked for [divisibility](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisibility) by $p$. If $p$ divides it, the [algorithm](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/algorithm) divides by $p$ repeatedly until the factor is gone:

$$N_k=p^{v_p(N_k)}\cdot M_k,\qquad p\nmid M_k.$$

That repeated division matters because values such as $325=5^2\cdot 13$ contain higher powers. Processing the complete $p$-adic valuation ensures the remaining cofactor is always correct after the sweep for $p$.

### Step 4: Track the Largest Processed Prime Factor

The [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) sweep runs in increasing order. For each $k$, the implementation stores two pieces of information:

$$\text{current remainder of }N_k,\qquad \text{largest processed prime that has divided }N_k.$$

Because the [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) are handled from small to large, whenever a new [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) divides the current remainder it automatically becomes the largest processed [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) seen so far for that $k$. After all eligible small [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) have been removed, the [largest prime factor](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/largest-prime-factor) is simply the larger of those two stored quantities.

### Step 5: Why Scanning Primes Only up to $2K$ Is Enough

The implementations generate [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) only up to $2K$. This is sufficient because every [prime factor](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-factor) $p\le 2K$ of any $N_k$ is removed during the [number-theoretic sieve](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-sieve). After that, the remaining cofactor has no [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) [divisor](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) $\le 2K$.

Suppose the leftover cofactor were composite and greater than $1$. Then it would have at least two [prime factors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-factor), both strictly larger than $2K$, so it would be at least

$$(2K+1)^2=4K^2+4K+1,$$

which is larger than every value in the family because

$$4k^2+1\le 4K^2+1.$$

This contradiction shows that the leftover is either $1$ or one [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime). Hence the final answer for each $k$ is

$$P(k)=\max(\text{largest processed prime for }k,\ \text{leftover cofactor for }k).$$

### Worked Example: The First Ten Terms

For $K=10$, the values are small enough to inspect directly:

$$\begin{aligned}
  4(1)^2+1&=5 &&\Rightarrow P(1)=5,\\
  4(2)^2+1&=17 &&\Rightarrow P(2)=17,\\
  4(3)^2+1&=37 &&\Rightarrow P(3)=37,\\
  4(4)^2+1&=65=5\cdot 13 &&\Rightarrow P(4)=13,\\
  4(5)^2+1&=101 &&\Rightarrow P(5)=101,\\
  4(6)^2+1&=145=5\cdot 29 &&\Rightarrow P(6)=29,\\
  4(7)^2+1&=197 &&\Rightarrow P(7)=197,\\
  4(8)^2+1&=257 &&\Rightarrow P(8)=257,\\
  4(9)^2+1&=325=5^2\cdot 13 &&\Rightarrow P(9)=13,\\
  4(10)^2+1&=401 &&\Rightarrow P(10)=401.
  \end{aligned}$$

Therefore

$$S(10)=5+17+37+13+101+29+197+257+13+401=1070.$$

This is also a useful checkpoint for the implementation. The [number-theoretic sieve](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-sieve) interpretation is visible here: the [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) $5$ hits the [residue classes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/residue-class) $k\equiv 1,4\pmod 5$, so among the first ten indices it strips factors from $k=1,4,6,9$.

## How the Code Works

The C++, Python, and Java implementations all follow the same pipeline. First they build all [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) up to $2K$ with a [linear sieve](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/linear-sieve). Then they initialize an array containing the values $4k^2+1$ and a second array that stores the [largest prime factor](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/largest-prime-factor) already confirmed during the sweep.

For each [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) $p\equiv 1\pmod 4$, the implementation computes a [square root](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/square-root) of $-1$ [modulo](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modulo) $p$, converts that root into the two valid [residue classes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/residue-class) of $k$, and walks those [arithmetic progressions](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/arithmetic-progression) with step $p$. At each visited index, if the current remainder is divisible by $p$, the code divides out all powers of $p$ and updates the stored largest processed [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime).

After every eligible [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) up to $2K$ has been handled, each index $k$ has a remaining cofactor that is either $1$ or [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime). The implementation takes the maximum of that leftover value and the largest processed [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime), adds it to the running total, and reduces the sum [modulo](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modulo) $10^{18}$.

## Complexity Analysis

Generating all [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) up to $2K$ with a [linear sieve](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/linear-sieve) costs $O(K)$ time and $O(K)$ memory. The residue-class sweeps contribute about

$$\sum_{\substack{p\le 2K\\ p\equiv 1\!\!\!\pmod 4}} \frac{2K}{p},$$

which has the usual near-harmonic [number-theoretic sieve](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-sieve) growth, so the overall running time is $O(K\log\log K)$ in practice. The repeated divisions by [prime powers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-power) do not change that asymptotic bound, and the dominant storage remains the two arrays of length $K+1$, so the memory usage is $O(K)$.
