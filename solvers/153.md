# Project Euler 153 Solution - Investigating Gaussian Integers

<https://projecteuler.net/problem=153>:

* [153.py](153.py)

## Problem Summary

Let $s(n)$ denote the Gaussian-divisor sum defined in the problem, and let

$$S(N)=\sum_{n=1}^{N} s(n),\qquad N=10^8.$$

Ordinary positive [divisors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) of $n$ are also Gaussian [divisors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor), purely imaginary [divisors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) have [real part](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/real-part) $0$, and genuinely complex [divisors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) occur in conjugate pairs whose [imaginary parts](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/imaginary-part) cancel. The task is therefore not to factor every $n$ separately in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, but to count how often each possible Gaussian [divisor](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) appears among the [integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) up to $N$.

The C++, Python, and Java implementations all exploit the same reorganization: isolate the ordinary [divisor](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) contribution, classify every non-real Gaussian [divisor](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) by a primitive direction and an [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) scale, and evaluate the resulting sums through a fast summatory [divisor](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) routine.

## Mathematical Approach

The entire solution comes from turning a sum over [integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) $n$ into a sum over Gaussian [divisors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) that can divide some $n\le N$.

### Ordinary divisors versus genuine Gaussian divisors

The [divisors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) on the [real axis](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/real-axis) are exactly the ordinary positive [divisors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor). Their total contribution to $S(N)$ is

$$T(N)=\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sigma(m)=\sum_{d=1}^{N} d\left\lfloor\frac{N}{d}\right\rfloor,$$

because each [positive integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/positive-integer) $d$ divides exactly $\lfloor N/d\rfloor$ [integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) up to $N$. This is the familiar summatory [divisor function](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor-function).

Purely imaginary [divisors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) need no separate treatment: their [real part](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/real-part) is zero, so they do not affect the final total. The only additional work comes from Gaussian [divisors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) with both real and [imaginary parts](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/imaginary-part) nonzero.

### Primitive directions and scaled divisors

Every contributing non-real Gaussian [divisor](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) can be represented uniquely in the form

$d(a+bi)$ or $d(a-bi)$.

where $d\ge 1$, $a,b\ge 1$, and $\gcd(a,b)=1$. Any common factor of the real and [imaginary parts](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/imaginary-part) is absorbed into the [positive integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/positive-integer) scale $d$, so $(a,b)$ describes a primitive Gaussian direction.

For that primitive direction, define the norm

$$m=a^2+b^2.$$

This is exactly why the implementations loop only over [coprime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/coprime) pairs $(a,b)$: non-primitive pairs would merely duplicate [divisors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) already counted by a larger scale $d$.

### The divisibility test for ordinary integers

Fix a primitive direction $(a,b)$ and a scale $d$. To determine when $d(a+bi)$ divides an ordinary [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) $n$, multiply by the conjugate:

$$\frac{n}{d(a+bi)}=\frac{n(a-bi)}{d(a^2+b^2)}=\frac{n(a-bi)}{dm}.$$

Because $\gcd(a,b)=1$, we also have $\gcd(a,m)=\gcd(b,m)=1$. Therefore the real and [imaginary parts](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/imaginary-part) of the quotient are [integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) exactly when $dm\mid n$.

This is the crucial simplification. [Divisibility](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisibility) by a [Gaussian integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/gaussian-integer) is reduced to an ordinary [divisibility](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisibility) condition involving the scale $d$ and the primitive norm $m=a^2+b^2$.

### Collapsing the global sum

For fixed $(a,b)$ and $d$, the conjugate pair $d(a+bi)$ and $d(a-bi)$ contributes the same [real part](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/real-part) $da$, so together they contribute $2da$ to every [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) $n$ divisible by $dm$. There are exactly $\left\lfloor N/(dm)\right\rfloor$ such [integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) up to $N$.

Hence one primitive direction $(a,b)$ contributes

$$\sum_{d\ge 1} 2da\left\lfloor\frac{N}{dm}\right\rfloor=2a\sum_{d\le N/m} d\left\lfloor\frac{N/m}{d}\right\rfloor=2a\,T\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac{N}{m}\right\rfloor\right).$$

Adding the ordinary [divisor](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) part gives the exact formula implemented by all three languages:

$$\boxed{S(N)=T(N)+\sum_{\substack{a,b\ge 1\\ \gcd(a,b)=1\\ a^2+b^2\le N}} 2a\,T\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac{N}{a^2+b^2}\right\rfloor\right).}$$

The condition $a^2+b^2\le N$ is automatic: if the primitive norm already exceeds $N$, then even the unscaled Gaussian [divisor](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) cannot divide any [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) at most $N$.

### Worked example: the primitive pair $1+2i$

Take $a=1$, $b=2$, so $m=1^2+2^2=5$. If $N=20$, then the relevant scaled [divisors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) are

$$1\pm 2i,\quad 2\pm 4i,\quad 3\pm 6i,\quad 4\pm 8i,$$

because $d\le N/m=4$. The pair $d(1\pm 2i)$ divides exactly the multiples of $5d$, and its total real contribution is $2d\cdot \lfloor 20/(5d)\rfloor$. Summing over $d=1,2,3,4$ gives

$$2\bigl(1\cdot 4+2\cdot 2+3\cdot 1+4\cdot 1\bigr)=30.$$

This is precisely

$$2a\,T\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac{20}{5}\right\rfloor\right)=2\,T(4),$$

which shows on a small example how one primitive direction collapses to one summatory-divisor query.

## How the Code Works

### Fast evaluation of the divisor-prefix function

The first ingredient is $T(M)=\sum_{d\le M} d\lfloor M/d\rfloor$. The implementation does not iterate over all $d$ one by one. Instead it groups together intervals on which the quotient $\lfloor M/d\rfloor$ is constant.

If the current left endpoint is $l$, then $q=\lfloor M/l\rfloor$ stays unchanged up to $r=\lfloor M/q\rfloor$. The whole block contributes

$$q\sum_{d=l}^{r} d=q\cdot \frac{(l+r)(r-l+1)}{2}.$$

This standard equal-quotient grouping reduces one prefix query from linear time to about $O(\sqrt M)$.

### Enumerating primitive Gaussian directions

The implementations start the answer with the ordinary [divisor](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) contribution $T(N)$. They then scan all [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) pairs $a\ge 1$, $b\ge 1$ satisfying $a^2+b^2\le N$, and retain only the [coprime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/coprime) pairs. Those are the primitive directions from the derivation above.

For each surviving pair, the norm $a^2+b^2$ is computed, the value $T\!\left(\left\lfloor N/(a^2+b^2)\right\rfloor\right)$ is queried, multiplied by $2a$, and added to the running total.

### Avoiding repeated work

Many different primitive pairs produce the same quotient $\left\lfloor N/(a^2+b^2)\right\rfloor$. The C++ and Python implementations store previously computed divisor-prefix values so that repeated queries are answered immediately. The Java implementation applies the same formula directly without that [memoization](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/memoization) layer, but the mathematical accumulation is identical in all three languages.

## Complexity Analysis

The dominant scan is the enumeration of [lattice points](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/lattice-point) in the quarter-disk $a^2+b^2\le N$, which contains $O(N)$ [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) pairs because the [radius](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/radius) is $\sqrt N$. The [coprimality](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/coprimality) test changes only the constant factor.

Each distinct divisor-prefix query is evaluated in about $O(\sqrt M)$ time by equal-quotient grouping. In the memoized variants, many queries reuse an earlier result because the same quotient occurs repeatedly. [Memory usage](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/memory-usage) is $O(U)$ for the number $U$ of cached prefix values; aside from that cache, the [algorithm](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/algorithm) uses only constant extra loop state.

The real gain is structural rather than cosmetic: instead of factoring every [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) up to $10^8$ in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, the solution performs one global scan of primitive Gaussian directions and a manageable number of summatory-divisor evaluations.
