# Project Euler 124 Solution - Ordered Radicals

<https://projecteuler.net/problem=124>:

* [124.py](124.py)

## Problem Summary

For each [positive integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/positive-integer) $n$, define

$$\operatorname{rad}(n)=\prod_{p\mid n} p,$$

the [product](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/product) of the distinct [prime divisors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-factor) of $n$, with $\operatorname{rad}(1)=1$. The problem orders all [integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) $1 \le n \le 100{,}000$ by the pair $(\operatorname{rad}(n),n)$: smaller [integer radical](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/radical-of-an-integer) first, and if two numbers have the same [integer radical](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/radical-of-an-integer), the smaller [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) comes first. If $E(k)$ denotes the $k$-th term of that ordered list, the goal is to determine $E(10000)$.

The only real computational issue is that the order depends on all $100{,}000$ [integer radical](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/radical-of-an-integer) values. The implementations avoid factoring each number separately. Instead, they compute every [integer radical](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/radical-of-an-integer) in one sieve-like pass and then perform a single [sort](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/sorting) using the exact order required by the statement.

## Mathematical Approach

The structure of the problem is simple but very specific: compute $\operatorname{rad}(n)$ for every $n$, [sort](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/sorting) the resulting pairs, and read one position. The mathematical content lies in finding the right way to build the whole [integer radical](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/radical-of-an-integer) table efficiently.

### The ordering that defines $E(k)$

Write

$$a \prec b \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \bigl(\operatorname{rad}(a),a\bigr) \lt \bigl(\operatorname{rad}(b),b\bigr)$$

in [lexicographic order](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/lexicographic-order). Then $E(1),E(2),\dots,E(100000)$ is just the list of [integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) $1,2,\dots,100000$ sorted by $\prec$. This formulation matters because ties are not arbitrary: if $\operatorname{rad}(a)=\operatorname{rad}(b)$, then the smaller of $a$ and $b$ must appear first.

### Radicals discard exponents

If

$$n=\prod_{i=1}^{r} p_i^{e_i},$$

then

$$\operatorname{rad}(n)=\prod_{i=1}^{r} p_i.$$

So the [integer radical](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/radical-of-an-integer) remembers which [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-number) divide $n$, but forgets how many times they occur. For example, $2$, $4$, $8$, and $16$ all have [integer radical](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/radical-of-an-integer) $2$; likewise $3$ and $9$ both have [integer radical](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/radical-of-an-integer) $3$. This is why powers of small [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-number) move forward in the ordered list even when the numbers themselves are larger. It also gives the standard inequality

$$\operatorname{rad}(n)\le n,$$

with equality exactly when $n$ is [squarefree](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/square-free-integer).

### A sieve that multiplies each prime exactly once

The key observation is that the [integer radical](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/radical-of-an-integer) of $m$ is obtained by multiplying together the distinct [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-number) dividing $m$. Therefore one may initialize an [array](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/array) $R$ by $R[n]=1$ for all $n$, and then for each [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-number) $p \le N$ multiply $p$ into every multiple of $p$:

$$R[m]\leftarrow R[m]\cdot p \qquad \text{for } m=p,2p,3p,\dots.$$

Because the loop advances in steps of $p$, each multiple receives the factor $p$ exactly once, no matter whether $p$ divides it once or many times. After all [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-number) have been processed, every distinct [prime divisor](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-factor) of $m$ has contributed one factor, so

$$R[m]=\prod_{p\mid m} p=\operatorname{rad}(m).$$

The [primality test](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/primality-test) used by the implementations is also sieve-based: a boolean [array](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/array) marks composites as soon as they appear as nontrivial [multiples](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer-multiple) of a smaller [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-number). When the outer loop reaches an unmarked value, that value is [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-number) and should be propagated through its [multiples](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer-multiple).

### Why the sieve is correct

An easy [mathematical invariant](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/invariant-mathematics) proves correctness. After finishing all [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-number) up to some threshold $q$, the current value $R[n]$ equals the [product](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/product) of those [prime divisors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-factor) of $n$ that are at most $q$. Initially this is true with an [empty product](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/empty-product). When the next [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-number) $p$ is processed, exactly the [multiples](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer-multiple) of $p$ are updated, so each such number gains the missing factor $p$, and all other numbers remain correct. By [induction](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/induction), after the final [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-number) the full [integer radical](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/radical-of-an-integer) has been built for every $n$.

### Worked example: the range $1$ through $10$

For the first ten [integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer), the [integer radicals](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/radical-of-an-integer) are

$$\operatorname{rad}(1)=1,\ \operatorname{rad}(2)=2,\ \operatorname{rad}(3)=3,\ \operatorname{rad}(4)=2,\ \operatorname{rad}(5)=5,$$

$$\operatorname{rad}(6)=6,\ \operatorname{rad}(7)=7,\ \operatorname{rad}(8)=2,\ \operatorname{rad}(9)=3,\ \operatorname{rad}(10)=10.$$

[Sorting](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/sorting) the pairs $(\operatorname{rad}(n),n)$ gives

$$ (1,1),\ (2,2),\ (2,4),\ (2,8),\ (3,3),\ (3,9),\ (5,5),\ (6,6),\ (7,7),\ (10,10). $$

Hence

$$E(4)=8.$$

This example shows both parts of the ordering: $4$ and $8$ move ahead because their [integer radical](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/radical-of-an-integer) is only $2$, and ties among equal [integer radicals](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/radical-of-an-integer) are broken by the ordinary size of the [integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer).

### From the sorted list to the requested term

Once all [integer radicals](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/radical-of-an-integer) are known, the rest of the problem is purely an ordering step. Build the sortable collection, order it lexicographically by $(\operatorname{rad}(n),n)$, and take the $10000$-th entry. There is no hidden [recurrence relation](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/recurrence-relation) or deeper search after the [number-theoretic sieve](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-sieve); the whole solution is exactly this [integer radical](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/radical-of-an-integer) table plus one [sort](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/sorting).

## How the Code Works

The C++, Python, and Java implementations all follow the same three-stage [algorithm](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/algorithm).

### Building the radical table

An [array](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/array) of length $N+1$ is initialized with $1$. A second boolean [array](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/array) is used to recognize [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-number) during the sweep from $2$ to $N$. Whenever the current value is still unmarked, it is [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-number), so the implementation multiplies that [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-number) into every multiple and marks the nontrivial [multiples](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer-multiple) as composite for later iterations.

### Sorting by the exact problem key

After the [number-theoretic sieve](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-sieve), the implementation creates a collection representing all [integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) $1$ through $100{,}000$ together with their [integer radicals](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/radical-of-an-integer), or equivalently sorts the [integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) directly by the key $(\operatorname{rad}(n),n)$. The secondary key is essential; without it, equal-radical groups would not match the required definition of $E(k)$.

### Reading the target position

The final answer is the element in position $10000$ under 1-based counting, which is [array index](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/array-index) $9999$ under 0-based storage. One of the implementations also checks the miniature example above to confirm that the ordering rule produces $E(4)=8$ when the limit is $10$.

## Complexity Analysis

Let $N=100{,}000$. The [number-theoretic sieve](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-sieve) phase visits each multiple of each [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-number) once, so its running time is

$$\sum_{p\le N}\left\lfloor \frac{N}{p}\right\rfloor = O\!\left(N\sum_{p\le N}\frac{1}{p}\right)=O(N\log\log N).$$

[Sorting](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/sorting) $N$ items by $(\operatorname{rad}(n),n)$ costs $O(N\log N)$, which is the dominant term here. The memory usage is $O(N)$: one [array](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/array) for [integer radical](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/radical-of-an-integer) values, one boolean [array](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/array) for the [number-theoretic sieve](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-sieve) state, and one sortable collection containing the [integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) or the corresponding pairs.
