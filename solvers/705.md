# Project Euler 705 Solution - Total Inversion Count of Divided Sequences

<https://projecteuler.net/problem=705>:

* [705.py](705.py)

This solution computes

- `G(N)`: the concatenation of all [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) `< N` in increasing order, with every `0` [digit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) removed
- `F(N)`: the sum of [inversion counts](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/inversion-number) over **all** “divided sequences” obtained by replacing each [digit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) by one of its [divisors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor)

All results are taken [modulo](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modulo) `1_000_000_007`.

## Key ideas

### 1) Aggregate inversion totals
For any [digit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) sequence `x[0..L-1]`, the [inversion count](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/inversion-number) is:

\[
\text{inv}(x) = \sum_{0 \le i < j < L} [x_i > x_j]
\]

Rather than generating every divided sequence, the solver keeps totals over all
generated prefixes.  Let:

- `T` be the number of divided prefixes,
- `I` be the sum of their [inversion counts](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/inversion-number),
- `A[v]` be the total number of occurrences of value `v` over all prefixes.

### 2) Streaming the next digit
For a master [digit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) `d`, the allowed values are its positive [divisors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) in
`1..9`, and `W[d]` is the number of such [divisors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor).

When processing a new master [digit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) `d`, every old prefix branches into
`W[d]` new prefixes.  For each allowed appended value `v`, the new [permutation inversions](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/inversion-of-a-permutation)
are all earlier values larger than `v`:

\[
\sum_{v\mid d}\sum_{u>v} A[u].
\]

The old inversion total and occurrence counters are multiplied by `W[d]`, and
the fresh last [digit](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) contributes `T` to each [divisor](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/divisor) value.

### 3) Chunked linear transforms
The per-digit update is linear in the state `(T, I, A[1], ..., A[9])`.  For a
base-10000 chunk, the composition has the compact form:

\[
\begin{aligned}
T' &= mT,\\
A'[v] &= mA[v] + b[v]T,\\
I' &= mI + \sum_v c[v]A[v] + eT.
\end{aligned}
\]

The solver precomputes these coefficients for every [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) `0..9999`, using
the nonzero [decimal digits](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/decimal-digit) of the chunk.  A [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) below `10^8` then needs at
most two transform applications: the high base-10000 chunk and the low chunk.

### 4) Fast prime generation
`G(10^8)` is long, so [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) are generated with a **[segmented odd-only sieve](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/odd-only-segmented-sieve)**:

- [number-theoretic sieve](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/number-theoretic-sieve) only [odd numbers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/odd-number) in each segment
- mark composites with slicing (`bytearray[start::step] = ...`) for speed
- stream [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) without storing them all

### 5) Avoiding per-prime string conversions
Converting millions of [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) to strings is expensive. Instead, each [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) is
split into base-10000 chunks:

- precompute the non-zero [decimal digits](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/decimal-digit) for every [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) `0..9999`
- precompute the [DP](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/dynamic-programming) transform for every such chunk
- for a [prime](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) `p`, apply the transforms for `divmod(p, 10000)` in order
- zero [digits](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/numerical-digit) naturally disappear from the transform
