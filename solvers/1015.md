# Project Euler 1015 Solution - Partisan Kayles

<https://projecteuler.net/problem=1015>:

* [1015.py](1015.py)

`solve(n, modulus=10**9)` counts fair rows, reduced modulo `modulus`.
The empty row counts once. The two steps are a reduction of the
[partisan game](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/partisan-game) and a
[generating function](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/generating-function)
that counts the resulting positions without storing rows.

## Decide who wins

Let `d` be the number of blue pins minus the number of red pins. Also consider
the [impartial game](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/impartial-game) in which
either player may remove only an adjacent mixed-colour pair. Cut at gaps and
between equal adjacent colours. The resulting maximal alternating blocks are
independent components of this game.

Let `g(L)` be the [Sprague-Grundy value](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/sprague-grundy-theorem)
of a block of length `L`. Removing a pair leaves lengths `i` and `L-2-i`, so

`g(0)=g(1)=0`,

`g(L)=mex{g(i) XOR g(L-2-i): 0 <= i <= L-2}`.

Here [mex](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/minimum-excluded-value) is the least
nonnegative integer absent from the set. Symmetry halves the number of
splits to inspect. The whole position has value `x`, the
[exclusive OR](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/exclusive-or) of its block values.

For the original game, the winner rule is:

* `d>0`: Blue wins, whichever player starts.
* `d<0`: Red wins, whichever player starts.
* `d=0, x!=0`: the first player wins.
* `d=0, x=0`: the second player wins.

This can be proved by [induction](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/mathematical-induction)
on the number of remaining pins, including positions with gaps. Suppose
`d>0`. Every Red move leaves `d>0` and passes the turn to Blue. If Blue
moves, a mixed pair preserves this positive lead. If there is no mixed
pair, Blue removes one blue pin: the new lead is still positive, or is
zero with `x=0` and Red to move. Thus Blue has a winning move and Red has
none. The case `d<0` is symmetric.

At `d=0`, removing one or two own-colour pins gives the opponent a positive
lead. Only mixed pairs can win. Their winning rule is precisely the
Sprague-Grundy rule: a nonzero XOR has a move to zero, and zero has no move
to zero. Consequently a row is fair exactly when it is balanced and `x=0`.
Odd lengths therefore contribute zero.

## Project onto XOR zero

Choose a power of two `H` greater than every nimber up to length `n`.
For each `h` from zero to `H-1`, give a block of length `L` the sign

`w_L = (-1)^popcount(h AND g(L))`.

Multiplying block signs gives the sign of their XOR. The
[character orthogonality](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/character-theory)
identity

`sum_h (-1)^popcount(h AND x) = H if x=0, otherwise 0`

lets us count fair rows by summing signed balanced-row counts and dividing
by `H`. This is the projection underlying the
[Walsh-Hadamard transform](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/walsh-hadamard-transform).
The nimbers are calculated rather than taken from a periodicity table;
the target requires 16 characters.

## Count balanced rows for one character

Use `z` for row length and a [Laurent series](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/laurent-series)
variable `y` for the blue-minus-red lead. Write

`E(z)=sum_{j>=1} w_{2j}*z^(2j)`,

`O(z)=sum_{j>=0} w_{2j+1}*z^(2j+1)`.

An even alternating block has lead zero and switches the starting colour of
the next block. An odd block has lead `+1` or `-1` and preserves that
starting colour. The switch rule follows because adjacent blocks meet at
equal colours. Thus the [transfer matrix](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/transfer-matrix-method)
for appending a block is

```text
M = [[y*O, E],
     [E, O/y]].
```

There are two possible starting colours. Summing all nonempty sequences of
blocks and then adding the single empty row gives

`F(z,y) = (1,1)*(I-M)^(-1)*(1,1)^T - 1`

`       = ((1+E)^2-O^2) / (1+O^2-E^2-O*(y+1/y))`.

Balance means extracting the [constant coefficient](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/coefficient-extraction)
in `y`. With `A=1+O^2-E^2`, the
[central binomial coefficient](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/central-binomial-coefficient)
identity gives

`[y^0] 1/(A-O*(y+1/y)) = 1/sqrt(A^2-4*O^2)`.

Factoring the expression under the square root simplifies the signed
balanced-row series to

`S(z) = sqrt(((1+E)^2-O^2) / ((1-E)^2-O^2))`.

Only even powers of `z` occur. Set `u=z^2` and `O=z*T(u)`, then compute the
coefficient of `u^(n/2)` in

`sqrt(((1+E(u))^2-u*T(u)^2) / ((1-E(u))^2-u*T(u)^2))`.

## Exact series arithmetic and memory

Let the numerator and denominator be `P` and `Q`, both with constant term
one. Compute the quotient `C=P/Q` using

`C_j=P_j-sum_{i=1}^j Q_i*C_{j-i}`.

Then compute the root `S`, whose constant term is one, using

`S_j=(C_j-sum_{i=1}^{j-1} S_i*S_{j-i})/2`.

All divisions are exact because the combinatorial derivation identifies
`S_j` with an integer signed count. The solver asserts this divisibility.
It uses exact [integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer), averages over
characters exactly, and only then applies the requested
[modulo operation](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modulo-operation). This also
handles even moduli without attempting to invert two modulo `10**9`.

For each coefficient, `abs(S_j) <= binom(2j,j)`, and the absolute coefficients
of `S^2` are bounded by `4^j`. At `n=1000`, intermediate integers therefore
have about 1000 bits. Arrays hold only 501 coefficients per series, and
characters are processed one at a time. The algorithm takes `O(H*n^2)`
integer operations and `O(n^2)` bits of coefficient storage. The target
PyPy run took under one second and less than 70 MiB of resident memory
under a 1 GiB virtual-memory limit.

The executable asserts both supplied counts, `f(6)` and `f(20)`. The naive
implementation instead [enumerates](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/brute-force-search)
every colour row and solves the original rules by
[minimax](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/minimax), with
[memoization](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/memoization) of the two occupied
bit sets and the player to move. It preserves gaps and uses neither the
winner reduction nor generating functions. Verification compares the two
`solve()` functions through length 10, checks the winner rule on all
gapped positions of length nine, and independently counts balanced rows
through length 20. The naive solver is intentionally for small inputs.
