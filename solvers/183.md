# Project Euler 183 Solution - Maximum Product of Parts

<https://projecteuler.net/problem=183>:

* [183.py](183.py)

## Problem Summary

For each [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) $n\ge 5$, we split $n$ into $k$ equal real parts, so each part is $n/k$, and we consider the product

$$M(n,k)=\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^k.$$

Among all [positive integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/positive-integer) $k$, we choose the value that maximizes this expression and call the resulting maximum $M(n)$. The function $D(n)$ is then defined by the [decimal expansion](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/decimal-expansion) of $M(n)$: if $M(n)$ is a [terminating decimal](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/terminating-decimal), we take $D(n)=-n$; otherwise we take $D(n)=+n$.

The task is to evaluate $D(n)$ for every $n$ from 5 through 10000 and add the results. The challenge is not large-number arithmetic; it is identifying the maximizing $k$ and deciding the terminating-decimal condition without ever expanding huge powers explicitly.

## Mathematical Approach

The whole solution rests on two independent facts. First, the maximizing number of equal parts is always extremely close to $n/e$. Second, after we reduce $n/k$ to lowest terms, only the [prime factors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-factor) of its [denominator](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/denominator) matter for decimal termination.

### Locating the maximizing number of parts

For fixed $n$, define

$$f_n(k)=\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^k.$$

Comparing such values directly is inconvenient, so we take [logarithms](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/logarithm) and study

$$\phi_n(x)=\log f_n(x)=x(\log n-\log x)$$

for real $x>0$. Differentiating gives

$$\phi_n'(x)=\log\!\left(\frac{n}{x}\right)-1,\qquad \phi_n''(x)=-\frac{1}{x} \lt 0.$$

Because the [second derivative](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/second-derivative) is always negative, $\phi_n$ is [strictly concave](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/strictly-concave), so it has a unique real maximum. Setting $\phi_n'(x)=0$ yields

$$x=\frac{n}{e}.$$

Therefore the maximizing [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) $k$ must be one of the [integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) nearest to $n/e$. In practice this means either rounding $n/e$ to the nearest [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer), or comparing the two neighbors $\left\lfloor n/e\right\rfloor$ and $\left\lfloor n/e\right\rfloor+1$ with [logarithms](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/logarithm).

### Why the denominator test is enough

Let $k_n$ be the maximizing [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) for $n$. Reduce the fraction $n/k_n$ to lowest terms:

$$\frac{n}{k_n}=\frac{a}{q},\qquad \gcd(a,q)=1.$$

Then

$$M(n)=\left(\frac{n}{k_n}\right)^{k_n}=\left(\frac{a}{q}\right)^{k_n}=\frac{a^{k_n}}{q^{k_n}}.$$

Since $\gcd(a,q)=1$, we also have $\gcd(a^{k_n},q^{k_n})=1$, so this fraction is already reduced. That means the reduced [denominator](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/denominator) of $M(n)$ is exactly $q^{k_n}$.

A [rational number](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/rational-number) has a [terminating decimal](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/terminating-decimal) expansion if and only if its reduced [denominator](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/denominator) has no [prime factors](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-factor) other than 2 and 5. Therefore $M(n)$ terminates exactly when $q$ itself has the form

$$q=2^\alpha 5^\beta$$

for some [integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) $\alpha,\beta\ge 0$. This is why the implementations only compute

$$q=\frac{k_n}{\gcd(n,k_n)}$$

and then repeatedly divide out factors of 2 and 5.

### Worked examples

Take $n=11$. Since $11/e\approx 4.046$, the only serious [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) candidates are $k=4$ and $k=5$. Their products are

$$\left(\frac{11}{4}\right)^4=57.19140625,\qquad \left(\frac{11}{5}\right)^5=51.53632,$$

so the maximum occurs at $k_{11}=4$. The [reduced fraction](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/reduced-fraction) is $11/4$, hence $q=4=2^2$. Therefore

$$M(11)=\left(\frac{11}{4}\right)^4=\frac{14641}{256}$$

has a [terminating decimal](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/terminating-decimal) expansion, and $D(11)=-11$.

For contrast, take $n=8$. Here $8/e\approx 2.943$, so the maximizing [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) is $k_8=3$. Then

$$\frac{8}{3}$$

is already reduced, so $q=3$. Because 3 is neither 2 nor 5, the [denominator](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/denominator) of $M(8)=(8/3)^3=512/27$ contains a forbidden [prime factor](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime-factor), so the [decimal expansion](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/decimal-expansion) does not terminate and $D(8)=+8$.

### The final summation

Once the sign rule is understood, the rest of the problem is completely separable by $n$. For each [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) $n\in[5,10000]$, we find the maximizing $k_n$, form $q=k_n/\gcd(n,k_n)$, test whether $q$ contains [primes](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/prime) other than 2 and 5, and contribute either $-n$ or $+n$. Symbolically,

$$\sum_{n=5}^{10000} D(n)$$

is computed as a simple loop with no interaction between different values of $n$.

## How the Code Works

The C++, Python, and Java implementations all follow the same mathematical pipeline, with a small difference in how they choose the optimal number of parts. The C++ implementation computes the two neighboring [integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) around $n/e$ and compares their logarithmic scores $k(\log n-\log k)$, which avoids constructing the power itself. The Python and Java implementations take the nearest [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) to $n/e$, which matches the same [concavity](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/concavity) argument.

After choosing that maximizing [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer), the implementation reduces the fraction $n/k$ indirectly by computing $k/\gcd(n,k)$. This is the reduced [denominator](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/denominator) $q$. It then removes every factor of 2 and every factor of 5 from $q$. If nothing remains, the maximal product has a [terminating decimal](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/terminating-decimal) expansion, so the running total is decreased by $n$. Otherwise the running total is increased by $n$.

No big-integer [exponentiation](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/exponentiation) is needed anywhere. The code never forms $M(n)$ itself, because the logarithmic comparison is enough to find the maximizing $k$, and the [denominator](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/denominator) criterion is enough to decide the sign of $D(n)$.

## Complexity Analysis

Each value of $n$ is processed independently. The work consists of one constant-size floating-point [optimization](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/optimization) step, one greatest-common-divisor computation, and a short loop that strips factors of 2 and 5 from the reduced [denominator](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/denominator). The [gcd](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/greatest-common-divisor) step costs $O(\log n)$, and the factor stripping is also $O(\log n)$ in the worst case.

If the upper limit is $U$, the total [running time](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/running-time) is $O(U\log U)$; for the specific range $5\le n\le 10000$, this is effectively a linear scan with very small constant factors. The auxiliary [memory usage](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/memory-usage) is $O(1)$.
