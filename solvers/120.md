# Project Euler 120 Solution - Square Remainders

<https://projecteuler.net/problem=120>:

* [120.py](120.py)

## Problem Summary

For each [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) $a$ with $3 \le a \le 1000$, define $r_n(a)$ to be the [remainder](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/remainder) when $(a-1)^n + (a+1)^n$ is divided by $a^2$. The problem asks for the largest possible [remainder](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/remainder) $r_{\max}(a)$ for each fixed $a$, and then for the [sum](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/sum) of these maxima over the whole range. The important point is that the [exponent](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/exponent) $n$ is not fixed: we are free to choose the value of $n$ that makes the [remainder](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/remainder) as large as possible.

A [brute-force search](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/brute-force-search) over many [exponents](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/exponent) would miss the main structure. Once the expression is reduced [modulo](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modulo-operation) $a^2$, almost the entire binomial expansion disappears, and the problem collapses to a short [integer parity](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer-parity) argument together with a small modular-arithmetic case split on whether $a$ is odd or even.

## Mathematical Approach

The quantity to analyze is

$$r_n(a) \equiv (a-1)^n + (a+1)^n \pmod{a^2}.$$

The whole solution comes from understanding which terms of the two powers can survive [modulo](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modulo-operation) $a^2$.

### Binomial collapse modulo $a^2$

Expand both powers around $\pm 1$:

$$
  (a+1)^n = 1 + na + \binom{n}{2}a^2 + \cdots,
  $$

$$
  (a-1)^n = (-1)^n + n(-1)^{n-1}a + \binom{n}{2}(-1)^{n-2}a^2 + \cdots.
  $$

[Modulo](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modulo-operation) $a^2$, every term containing $a^2$ or a higher power vanishes. Only the constant and linear terms matter, so

$$
  (a+1)^n \equiv 1 + na \pmod{a^2},
  \qquad
  (a-1)^n \equiv (-1)^n + n(-1)^{n-1}a \pmod{a^2}.
  $$

Adding them gives a complete reduction:

$$
  r_n(a) \equiv
  \begin{cases}
  2 \pmod{a^2}, & n \text{ even},\\
  2an \pmod{a^2}, & n \text{ odd}.
  \end{cases}
  $$

So the enormous-looking expression is really governed by only two pieces of data: the [integer parity](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer-parity) of $n$, and, for odd $n$, the residue of $2n$ [modulo](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modulo-operation) $a$.

### Why only odd exponents can produce the maximum

When $n$ is even, the [remainder](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/remainder) is always exactly $2$. For $a \ge 3$, the values obtained from odd [exponents](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/exponent) are much larger, because they are [multiples](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer-multiple) of $a$. In fact, once $n$ is odd we can write

$$
  r_n(a) = a \cdot (2n \bmod a),
  $$

where the factor $2n \bmod a$ is taken in the range $0,1,\dots,a-1$. Therefore maximizing the [remainder](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/remainder) is equivalent to maximizing the reachable residue $2n \bmod a$ while keeping $n$ odd.

### The residue pattern for odd $n$

Let $n=2k+1$. Then

$$
  2n \equiv 4k+2 \pmod a.
  $$

As $k$ increases by 1, this residue advances by $4$ [modulo](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modulo-operation) $a$. That turns the original problem into a very small orbit question in the ring $\mathbb{Z}/a\mathbb{Z}$.

If $a$ is odd, then $\gcd(4,a)=1$. Repeatedly adding $4$ [modulo](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/modulo-operation) $a$ visits every [residue class](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/residue-class), so among odd [exponents](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/exponent) we can reach every possible value from $0$ to $a-1$. In particular, the largest residue $a-1$ is attainable, and therefore

$$
  r_{\max}(a) = a(a-1) \qquad (a \text{ odd}).
  $$

If $a$ is even, then $2n$ is always even, so the residue $a-1$ can never occur. The largest possible residue below $a$ with the correct [integer parity](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer-parity) is $a-2$. It is actually attained: choosing $n=a-1$, which is odd, gives $2n=2a-2 \equiv a-2 \pmod a$. Hence

$$
  r_{\max}(a) = a(a-2) \qquad (a \text{ even}).
  $$

### Closed form for the maximum remainder

The case split can be written compactly as

$$
  r_{\max}(a)=
  \begin{cases}
  a(a-2), & a \text{ even},\\
  a(a-1), & a \text{ odd}.
  \end{cases}
  $$

This is exactly the formula used by the implementations. Once it has been proved, the original number-theory problem becomes a direct [summation](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/summation) over $a=3,4,\dots,1000$.

### Worked examples

Take $a=7$. For odd [exponents](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/exponent), the coefficient $2n \bmod 7$ runs through

$$
  2,6,3,0,4,1,5,\dots
  $$

so the largest reachable value is $6$. Therefore $r_{\max}(7)=7\cdot 6=42$.

Now take $a=8$. For odd [exponents](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/exponent), the coefficient $2n \bmod 8$ runs through

$$
  2,6,2,6,\dots
  $$

Only even residues occur, and the best one is $6=8-2$. Thus $r_{\max}(8)=8\cdot 6=48$. These two examples show exactly why the final formula depends only on the [integer parity](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer-parity) of $a$.

## How the Code Works

The C++, Python, and Java implementations do not search over [exponents](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/exponent) at all. They rely on the [closed form](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/closed-form-expression) above, loop over every [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) $a$ from $3$ to $1000$, determine whether $a$ is odd or even, and add the corresponding contribution $a(a-1)$ or $a(a-2)$ to a running total.

This means the code is already working at the final mathematical level. All of the difficult reasoning is pushed into the derivation of $r_{\max}(a)$; once that derivation is known, the implementation is just a short accumulation over 998 values of $a$.

## Complexity Analysis

There is one pass through the [integers](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) $a=3$ to $1000$, and each iteration performs only a [integer parity](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer-parity) test, two small [multiplications](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/multiplication), and one [addition](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/addition). The running time is therefore $O(1000)$, or more generally $O(A)$ if the [upper bound](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/upper-bound) is $A$. The memory usage is $O(1)$, since the implementation stores only the running total and a few temporary [integer](https://ourbigbook.com/codex/integer) values.
